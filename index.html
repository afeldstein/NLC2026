Beyond the Productivity Trap

NLC 2026 Roundtable — Andrew Feldstein, D.P.S. — Fort Hays State University

Card 1 Disruption
The Productivity Trade-Off
"AI makes teams faster. It also makes them quieter. If the silence is where trust, negotiation, and mutual understanding used to live, what did we gain?"
The Finding
In a large-scale experiment with 2,310 participants, human-AI teams produced 50% more output per worker. But human-human teams engaged in 29% more social and emotional communication — greetings, encouragement, rapport-building, self-disclosure. The researchers called the reduction "decreased social coordination effort."
Ju, H., & Aral, S. (2025). Collaborating with AI agents. arXiv:2503.18238.
Card 2 Disruption
The Network Collapse
"The internet let us build our own networks instead of relying on the ones that formed naturally through proximity. AI lets us skip the networks entirely. In five of six collaborative groups given a GAI chatbot, students directed more messages to the chatbot than to each other — not as a shared resource, but individually. No one needed to ask a classmate."
The Finding
Network analysis confirmed statistically significant GAI-centered interaction patterns in five of six groups. Students who heavily used the chatbot did not maintain strong connections with each other. The chatbot became the dominant hub; peer-to-peer relay functions migrated to it.
Feng, S. (2025). Group interaction patterns in generative AI-supported collaborative problem solving. British Journal of Educational Technology, 56(5), 2125–2145.
Card 3 Disruption
The Satisfaction Paradox
"The students who used AI chatbots the most and liked them the most were also the most worried about losing human connection. They could name what was disappearing. They used it anyway."
The Finding
A quantitative study of higher education students found that higher levels of chatbot experience and greater satisfaction with chatbot interactions were positively correlated with increased concerns about the loss of human connection and emotional support.
Al-Zahrani, A. M. (2025). Exploring the impact of artificial intelligence chatbots on human connection and emotional support. SAGE Open, 15(1).
Card 4 Disruption
The Epistemic Gap
"AI agents don't just answer questions — they become the place where judgment was supposed to develop. The task gets done. The capacity to evaluate it does not."
The Finding
In GAI-centered collaborative groups, student-chatbot exchanges were overwhelmingly cognitive — task questions, concept clarification, solution requests. Students asked the chatbot to evaluate their proposed solutions rather than developing evaluative capacity through peer exchange. Metacognitive interactions lacked critical depth — consistent with what a separate study of AI-supported learning calls "metacognitive laziness": students offloading reflective thinking to AI rather than doing it themselves or with peers.
Feng, S. (2025). British Journal of Educational Technology, 56(5), 2125–2145. Fan, Y., et al. (2024). Beware of metacognitive laziness. British Journal of Educational Technology, 56(2), 489–530.
Card 5 Design
The Design Alternative
"What if AI never answered a student's question — and instead asked a classmate to?"
The Finding
A machine-learning-based conversational agent called Clair was embedded in an inquiry learning platform's chat. Rather than answering questions, it analyzed student dialogue using over 20 contextual variables to identify the right moment to intervene — then prompted students to build on each other's reasoning, revoice peers' ideas, and connect contributions to domain concepts. The result: significant increases in productive peer dialogue, including students engaging with each other's reasoning.
de Araujo, R. D., et al. (2024). A learning analytics-based collaborative conversational agent to foster productive dialogue in inquiry learning. Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 40(6).
Card 6 Design
The Role Reversal
"When the AI knew less than the students, the students explained more to each other. When the AI knew more, they stopped talking."
The Finding
A study comparing conversational agents in different roles found that when the agent was positioned as a less-knowledgeable peer, student groups more frequently posed questions, tested the agent's understanding, and built on prior exchanges to explain concepts to each other. The expert-positioned agent produced dependency; the peer-positioned agent produced dialogue.
Nguyen, H. (2023). Role design considerations of conversational agents. Computers & Education, 192, 104661.
Card 7 Limits
The Listening Illusion
"AI listened better than most humans. Participants felt heard, felt free to express themselves, felt connected. But they were just as lonely afterward as when they started. The experience of connection was real. The connection was not."
The Finding
Across three experiments (N = 668), AI prompted to provide high-quality listening was rated as better than participants' average human interaction. It satisfied psychological needs for autonomy and relatedness. But it did not reduce loneliness — not even for participants highest in trait loneliness. AI created the experience of a supportive relationship without producing the relational benefits that human listening provides.
Weinstein, N., Itzchakov, G., & Maniaci, M. R. (2025). Exploring the connecting potential of AI. Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans, 4, 100149.
